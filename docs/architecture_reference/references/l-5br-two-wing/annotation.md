## Why this plan works

- Two wings joined at a seam, with circulation crossing the seam through a hall, is the census's dominant pattern for the 13 of 21 reference plans that weren't simple rectangles; this entry represents the majority non-rectangular case, not an outlier shape. [E]
- Putting the hall specifically at the seam gives the plan one legible crossing point between wings rather than requiring circulation to zigzag between them at multiple points. [D]
- Five bedrooms and three bathrooms split across two wings keeps each wing's own corridor short, rather than one corridor long enough to reach all five bedrooms in sequence. [D]

## Trade-offs

- A two-wing seam plan needs the hall at the seam to actually work as circulation; if the seam hall is undersized or off-centre relative to both wings, the whole plan's topology quality depends on that one junction holding up, more so than a single-rectangle plan's circulation depends on any one point. [E]

## Anti-patterns avoided

- Routing every wing-to-wing trip through one hall at the seam, rather than through a chain of intervening rooms, avoids [Chained circulation](../../anti_patterns.md#11-chained-circulation).
